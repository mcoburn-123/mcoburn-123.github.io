What the regulars know

The job aid. Tape it inside the supply closet door.

  1. A first-timer gets a name tag, one job and a buddy before anything else happens.
  2. A big group gets split into fours. Each four works under someone who's been here before.
  3. Two minutes on safety before the first box moves: lifting, gloves, cold food, and where the first aid kit is. Your food bank's rules win wherever they're stricter.
  4. Before anyone gets their coat, thank them for one real thing they did and book their next date on the spot.
  5. None of this is free. Decide what waits while you do it.
Why I designed it this way

The problem. A new pantry volunteer usually decides during the first shift whether to come back, and the lead running that shift is slammed.

The learners. Volunteer leads at pantries and meal programs. They're on their feet, short on time, and usually on a phone.

What a lead practices. Welcoming a first-timer with a name, one job and a buddy. Splitting a big group into small crews led by returning volunteers. Giving a two-minute safety talk before the work starts. Closing by booking the next date on the spot.

Why trade-offs. A lead's calls rarely have a right answer. Each one here feeds one meter and costs another, and the game shows what happened without grading it. The scenes come from a deck, so a second play is a different shift.

Why it skips ahead a week. A welcome or a booked date pays off seven days later, when someone does or doesn't walk back down the stairs. So that's where the ending is.

Accessibility. Play by tap, click or number keys. Nothing is timed. Meter changes show a sign and a count as well as a color. Reduced-motion settings turn the animation off, and sound stays off until you turn it on.

Evaluation plan (not yet piloted). Level 1: a two-question reaction check after play. Level 2: leads talk through their calls on fresh scenes. Level 3: compare how many new volunteers come back for leads who played versus leads who didn't, using the food bank's own sign-up data.
